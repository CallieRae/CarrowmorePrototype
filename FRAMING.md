# Framing the bet

A keeper's working life is already written down, just not in a language employers read. The bet: forty years of logbooks can be translated into an employer's terms, graded honestly, so a keeper sees what he is qualified for, what he is nearly qualified for, and the shortest route between the two.

## Whose problem

The keeper's: *"What am I qualified for, what am I nearly qualified for, and what is the shortest honest route from here to a job?"* The redeployment shortlist runs on the same evidence, so one record serves both views.

## What I believe but cannot yet prove

1. Skills are what you know, not how you learned them: "isolated no.2 and earthed off" is what a module assesses.
2. Translation is the value, even where the keeper already knows the skill.
3. Most keepers are nearly qualified: 23 of 38 hold some HV Authorized Person modules; 2 hold the full certificate.
4. Employers will weigh inferred evidence. Not testable this week.

## The one that sinks it

**AI can map log entries to employer requirements accurately enough, and word the result plainly enough, that a keeper acts on it correctly.** Wrong, or read as a promise, it sends a fifty-four-year-old to an interview he was never going to pass.

## How I tested it

Ten keepers, chosen by rule with three held out, were graded blind by hand on five requirements before the AI graded them, citing every log line. No match score: four evidence tiers, and logs never mark an insurer gate as met. The kill rules were committed first: beat keyword search on the held-out keepers, keep recall at 50% or above, and hold overclaims at 10% or below to persevere, 25% to pivot.

## What I chose not to build

The raw pack (tying entries to people took the Authority three weeks for 40 keepers; at 340, that is the kill at scale), a match percentage, four of the six postings, learning-rate and cost-of-candidacy models, appraisals (the tool seeks only what it needs) and real authentication (the mock sign-in shows who sees what).
